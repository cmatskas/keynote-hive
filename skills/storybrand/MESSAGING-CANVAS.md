THE 4 RULES
RULE #01 — Make it zero cognitive load 
Don't make people think.
Most businesses don’t realize they are talking over their 
customers' heads. It’s an easy mistake to make. If people have to 
do the slightest bit of thinking in order to understand what you 
are trying to say, they will move on. The effort to be clear, then, 
will beat the effort to be clever, every time. State your offer simply 
and plainly and customers will finally understand why they need 
your product, resulting in more orders.
      High cognitive load: “Adaptive multi-zone support 
      engineered with temperature-neutral foam technology.” 
      Low cognitive load: “A comfortable, cool bed for 
      better sleep.”

6
Short, simple phrases get repeated while complex and 
clever ideas die upon delivery. At StoryBrand, we call clever 
and complicated terms invisible words — that is, they blend 
into the sea of forgettable noise put out by most business 
leaders. After stating your message, ask yourself if there 
is anything you can do to make it more memorable and 
repeatable (like shorten it). If your message is memorable 
and repeatable, your customers won’t just place orders, their 
friends will place orders too.
      Forgettable: “Artisan, ethically sourced coffee 
      crafted by expert baristas.”
      Repeatable: “Coffee worth waking up for.”
      Weak survival link: “High-efficiency 
      monocrystalline panels with 22% conversion rates.”
      Strong survival link: “Cut your power bill in half.”
Human beings are always looking for survival assets. Why? 
Because the primary job of the human brain is to keep you, 
and the ones you love, alive and thriving. When you position 
your product or service as a survival asset, people begin to 
pay attention and tell their friends. Focus your message on 
the aspects of your products that help your customers get 
ahead, such as saving money, saving time, improving health, 
gaining status, building relationships, achieving greater 
security and helping your customer survive and thrive and 
they will turn to your products in their time of need.

7
Every human being self identifies as the hero in a story. 
When your brand positions itself as the hero, customers 
perceive you as being a survival liability because you are 
caught up in a story about yourself. Instead, play the guide. 
The guide is the stronger, more competent character in the 
story who is there to help the hero win. Katniss is the hero, 
Haymitch is the guide. Read the words on your website, pitch 
deck, and social media collateral. Is your message about you, 
or about your customers’ problems and why you exist to 
solve their problems? Make your story about the customer 
and they will pay attention.
These rules are simple to understand, but much harder to 
apply to your brand. Why? Because you’re too close to your 
own brand to see it clearly. It’s almost impossible to read 
the label from inside the jar. Consider one exercise as you 
proceed: Always ask yourself how your message could be 
misunderstood. If it can be misunderstood, clarify it further. 
Confusion will kill any brand. 
      Hero positioning: “Experience our historic property 
      features, celebrated architecture, and a four-star 
      kitchen.” 
      Guide positioning: “Unwind, sleep well, and wake 
      up somewhere you love.”

any other idea as it relates to your brand. It’s the single

To write your tagline, choose the one idea a distracted

20
When somebody asks the story of how your brand 
came to be, don’t tell them. I mean that. Instead, 
invite them into a story in which they play the hero 
and you play the role of guide. Why? Because that’s 
really what they’re asking for anyway. Potential 
customers are always wondering if you can help 
them and more, if you are competent to do so. Your 
brand story should reiterate the idea that the only 
reason you even exist is to solve your customers’ 
problems.
Believe it or not, new customers don’t really care that 
your grandfather started the company or you got 
into the business selling bananas before you started 
selling bicycles. What they are really wondering is 
how you can help them survive and whether your 
brand story backs up that fact.
To create your brand story you will combine all of 
your Foundational Soundbites, then edit them into 
one short paragraph. Your brand story will invite your 
potential customers into using your product to solve 
their problem in clear and compelling language.
You will use your brand story anywhere you’d 
normally talk about your company such as on your 
about page, in your keynote presentation, during 
your initial sales meetings, and so on. Again, though, 
there’s a switch. The whole time you’re telling your 
story, it’s secretly about the customer, not you. 
To be sure, after you get to know a customer and 
they’re already trusting you as their guide, telling 
your true backstory can be fun and can even build a 
deeper relationship, but don’t be fooled. If a customer 
is just getting to know you, don’t tell your story. 
Rather, invite them into a story.
To write your brand story, simply place your 5 
Soundbites in order and add the small connecting 
words that make them flow together as a short, 
cohesive narrative.

The triangle on your Messaging 
Canvas is there to remind you and 
your team that every customer goes 
through three phases as they build a 
relationship with your brand. Those three 
phases are: curiosity, enlightenment, 
and commitment. The first job of a 
messaging campaign, then, is to pique 
your customers’ curiosity. The second 
job is to enlighten them about how 
your product can help them solve their 
problem, and the third is to incentivize 
them to commit.
When buying a pack of gum, that journey will only 
take a second or two. If you’re selling a product that 
costs millions, however, the process could take years. 
The longer the buying journey, the more marketing 
and messaging collateral you will need, each piece, 
of course, repeating your foundational message thus 
building familiarity and trust over time.
Your message, and your map, are all located on 
the Messaging Canvas so that your campaign is 
consistent and cohesive.
The good news, again, is that you’re not starting from 
scratch. Your three cornerstone tools can be placed 
on your map immediately. Your tagline sits at the top 
of the map in the curiosity phase as it’s designed to 
pique your customers’ curiosity and make them want 
to know more. Your elevator pitch and your brand 
story begin to enlighten your customers about why 
and how you exist to solve their problems. You will 
likely repeat your tagline, elevator pitch, and brand 
story in the pitch decks, sales scripts, and other sales 
collateral that populate the commitment phase of 
your campaign.

Now that you’ve piqued your customer’s curiosity and built familiarity and trust, it’s time to ask 
for the sale. Commitment tools are designed specifically to ask your customer to commit to your 
brand. These asks need to be direct, because passive suggestions do not work. Commitment 
collateral can include: your call to action, a sales script, a discovery-call framework, proposals, 
and follow-up emails. Bonus incentives like limited-time discounts or expiring bonus offerings 
can also be used in this phase of the campaign. The important thing to remember is that, at 
this point, your customer actually wants to place an order, they are just looking for an excuse 
to do so. In the commitment phase, you want to give them plenty of great reasons to buy your 
product and buy it soon.
